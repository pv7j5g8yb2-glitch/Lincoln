Appeal of U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services (USCIS) Decision

Form I-918, Petition for U Nonimmigrant Status



The Petitioner seeks "U-1" nonimmigrant classification under sections 101(a)(15)(U) and 214(p) of
the Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act), 8 U.S .C. §§ 1101(a)(15)(U) and 1184(p).

USCIS denied the petition, concluding that the record did not establish that the Petitioner suffered
substantial physical or mental abuse as the result of being the victim of qualifying criminal activity.
The matter is now before us on appeal pursuant to 8 C.F.R. § 103.3.

The Petitioner bears the burden of proof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance of the evidence.
Matter ofChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter
de novo. Matter of Christo 's, Inc., 26 l&N Dec. 537, 537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review,
we will dismiss the appeal.

                                                        I. LAW

To establish eligibility for U-1 nonimmigrant classification, a petitioner must show that he or she, inter
alia, has suffered substantial physical or mental abuse as a result of having been the victim of
qualifying criminal activity. Section 101(a)(15)(U)(i) of the Act; 8 C.F.R. § 214.14(b)(l). Although
petitioners may submit any relevant, credible evidence for the agency to consider, USCIS determines,
in its sole discretion, the credibility of and weight given to such evidence. Section 214(p )(4) of the
Act; 8 C.F.R. § 214.14(c)(4).

B. Substantial Physical or Mental Abuse

Upon de novo review, the Petitioner has not submitted sufficient evidence to meet his burden of
showing by a preponderance of the evidence that he suffered substantial mental or physical abuse as
a result of the crime against him.

The Act and regulations provide that a petitioner is eligible for U-1 nonimmigrant status if he
demonstrates, inter alia, that he has suffered substantial physical or mental abuse as a result of having
been a victim of qualifying criminal activity. Section 101(a)(15)(U)(i)(I) of the Act; 8 C.F.R.
§ 214.14(b)(1 ). The regulations provide that the determination of whether a petitioner has suffered
substantial abuse is based on a number of factors, including but not limited to:

       The nature of the injury inflicted or suffered; the severity of the perpetrator's conduct;
       the severity of the harm suffered; the duration of the infliction of the harm; and the
       extent to which there is permanent or serious harm to the appearance, health, or
       physical or mental soundness of the victim, including aggravation of pre-existing
       conditions. No single factor is a prerequisite to establish that the abuse suffered was
       substantial. Also, the existence of one or more of the factors automatically does not
       create a presumption that the abuse suffered was substantial. A series of acts taken
       together may be considered to constitute substantial physical or mental abuse even
       where no single act alone rises to that level[.]

8 C.F.R. § 214.14(b)(l).

While we are sensitive to the Petitioner's victimization and recognize that he experienced fear during
and after the event, the record does not establish, by a preponderance of the evidence, that he has
suffered substantial physical or mental abuse as a result of the attempted robbery and assault
perpetrated against him. The police report reflects that the incident was of short duration, lasting only
one to three minutes from the time the suspects entered the restaurant until they left. The police report
also states that the suspects did not strike the Petitioner or his family members and that no injuries
occurred. The two Supplements B indicate that the Petitioner was frightened during the incident but


                                                   5
do not otherwise list any injuries or harm. Similarly, the urgent care records and worker's
compensation form shows that he was able to continue working without restrictions, and the
psychologist who treated him in 2012 noted that he and his family continued their scheduled work
shift that day after taking some time to calm down after the incident. This evidence shows that the
nature and severity of the crime and any injury suffered at the time were minor and that the duration
of infliction of harm was brief. 8 C.F.R. § 214.14(b)(l).

Furthermore, the evidence reflects that the psychological harm the Petitioner suffered as a result of the
crime was not severe. The Petitioner told the psychologist in 2012 that he had handled the incident
calmly and she determined that he had been able to "cope with it relatively well." Although she
concluded that he was "anxious in an expected fashion," she also noted that he "continue[ d] to engage
in previously enjoyable activities" and was only "somewhat worried" for his family members.
Overall, the psychologist in 2012 observed the Petitioner's "positive coping" and ability to support his
wife and son. Similarly, the psychologist who evaluated him in 2025 noted that the Petitioner was
"stressed, but ... believes it is all going to be ok" and that he had positive coping strategies, including
nature walks and talking to his wife, to "work out his stress [and] ... keep going and working."
Furthermore, the 2025 evaluation indicates that the Petitioner reported his wife's symptoms were
worse than his and that "his biggest problems" were her health and his immigration status rather than
any psychological symptoms ofhis own. The 2025 evaluation also reflects that although his numerical
score on a PTSD checklist was high, his own statements to the psychologist indicated a lower severity
of symptoms. We acknowledge the Petitioner's assertion on appeal that his PTSD diagnoses by the
urgent care doctor in 2012 and by the psychologist in 2025 indicate a long-term impact on his mental
health, but the diagnoses on their own are not sufficient to meet his burden when the details outlined
in the evidence otherwise reflect a less severe level of harm.

Additionally, the record contains inconsistencies regarding the details of the incident, including the
type of harm that occurred and its effect on the Petitioner. Although the Petitioner indicated in his
personal statement that both men had guns and that one pointed a gun at him while the other pointed
a gun at his son, the police report and security footage reflect that only one of the men held a gun.
And contrary to the Petitioner's statements to the psychologist in 2025 that the men hit him and his
family with guns and threatened to kill them, he did not mention hitting or threats in either of his
personal statements. The police report completed on the date of the incident also indicates that no
hitting occurred and does not mention any threats. Furthermore, although the Petitioner received a
prescription for anti-anxiety medication at the urgent care clinic in 2012, he told the psychologist in
2025 that he had not previously taken any psychiatric medications. The evidence about the severity
and duration of harm to the Petitioner's mental health is not consistent and is therefore insufficient to
show that it rises to the level of substantial as contemplated in section 10l(a)(l5)(U)(i)(I) of the Act
and 8 C.F.R. § 214.14(b)(l). 4

The evidence does not demonstrate by a preponderance of the evidence that the Petitioner suffered
substantial mental abuse as a result of qualifying criminal activity, as he claims. Section
10l(a)(l5)(U)(i)(I) of the Act; 8 C.F.R. § 214.14(b)(l). The U petition will remain denied.
