Appeal of U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services (USCIS) Decision

Form I-601 , Application for Waiver of Grounds oflnadmissibility



The Applicant seeks a waiver of inadmissibility under section 212(h) of the Immigration and
Nationality Act (the Act), 8 U.S.C. § 1182(h), based on two convictions for crimes involving moral
turpitude (CIMT) and section 212(i) of the Act, 8 U.S.C.§ 1182(i), for willful misrepresentation.
USCIS may grant this discretionary waiver if refusal of admission would result in extreme hardship
to a qualifying relative or qualifying relatives.

USCIS denied the Form I-601 , Application to Waive Inadmissibility Grounds (waiver application),
concluding that the record did not establish the requisite extreme hardship to the Applicant's U.S.
citizen spouse and child or that approval is warranted as a matter of discretion. The matter is now
before us on appeal pursuant to 8 C.F.R. § 103 .3.

The Applicant bears the burden ofproof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance of the evidence.
Matter ofChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter
de novo. Matter of Christa's, Inc. , 26 I&N Dec. 537, 537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review,
we will dismiss the appeal.

                                               I. LAW

An alien convicted of (or who admits having committed, or who admits committing acts which
constitute the essential elements of) a CIMT (other than a purely political offense) or an attempt or
conspiracy to commit such a crime is inadmissible. Section 212(a)(2)(A)(i) of the Act. Individuals
found inadmissible under section 212(a)(2)(A) of the Act for having committed a CIMT may seek a
discretionary waiver of inadmissibility under section 212(h) of the Act, 8 U.S.C. § 1182(h). Where
the activities resulting in inadmissibility occurred more than 15 years before the date of adjudication,
a waiver is available if admission to the United States would not be contrary to the national welfare,
safety, or security of the United States, and the alien has been rehabilitated. Section 212(h)(l)(A) of
the Act. A waiver is also available if denial of admission would result in extreme hardship to a United
States citizen or lawful permanent resident spouse, parent, son, or daughter. Section 212(h)(l)(B) of
the Act.
Any alien who, by fraud or willfully misrepresenting a material fact, seeks to procure (or has sought
to procure or has procured) a visa, other documentation, or admission into the United States or other
benefit provided under the Act, is inadmissible. Section 212(a)(6)(C)(i) of the Act. To establish
eligibility for a discretionary waiver of this inadmissibility the alien must demonstrate that denial of
admission will result in extreme hardship to their U.S. citizen or lawful permanent resident spouse, or
parent. Section 212(i) of the Act.

A determination of whether denial of admission will result in extreme hardship depends on the facts
and circumstances of each case. Matter of Cen;antes-Gonzalez, 22 I&N Dec. 560, 565 (BIA 1999)
(citations omitted). We recognize that some degree of hardship to qualifying relatives is present in
most cases; however, to be considered "extreme," the hardship must exceed that which is usual or
expected. See Matter of Pilch, 21 I&N Dec. 627, 630-31 (BIA 1996) (finding that factors such as
economic detriment, severing family and community ties, loss of current employment, and cultural
readjustment were the "common result of deportation" and did not alone constitute extreme hardship).
In determining whether extreme hardship exists, individual hardship factors that may not rise to the
level of extreme must also be considered in the aggregate. Matter of Ige, 20 I&N Dec. 880, 882
(BIA 1994) (citations omitted).

                                           II. ANALYSIS

USCIS determined the Applicant was inadmissible under section 212(a)(2)(A) of the Act for having
committed two CIMT convictions and section 212(a)(6)(C)(i) for willful misrepresentation of his
criminal history. On appeal, the Applicant does not contest his inadmissibility. The only issues on
appeal are whether the Applicant's qualifying relatives, his U.S. citizen spouse and child, would suffer
extreme hardship upon denial of the waiver application and, if so, whether he merits a favorable
exercise of discretion to grant the waiver.

An applicant may show extreme hardship in two scenarios: 1) if the qualifying relative remains in the
United States separated from the applicant and 2) if the qualifying relative relocates overseas with the
applicant. See generally 9 USCIS Policy Manual B.4(B), https://www.uscis.gov/po1icy-manua1
(providing guidance on the scenarios to consider in making extreme hardship determinations).
Demonstrating extreme hardship under both of these scenarios is not required if an applicant's
evidence establishes that one of these scenarios would result from the denial of the waiver. See id.
(citing to Matter of Calderon-Hernandez, 25 I&N Dec. 885 (BIA 2012) and Matter of Gonzalez
Recinas, 23 I&N Dec. 467 (BIA 2002)). An applicant may meet this burden by submitting a statement
from the qualifying relative certifying under penalty of perjury that the qualifying relative would
relocate with the applicant, or would remain in the United States, if the applicant is denied admission.
See generally 9 USCIS Policy Manual at B.4(B). The mere assertion of extreme hardship does not
establish a credible claim. See id. at B.6(B). Rather, each assertion should be accompanied by
evidence that substantively supports the claim absent a convincing explanation why the evidence is
unavailable and could not reasonably be obtained. See id. Here, the record does not contain a clear
statement from the Applicant's spouse indicating whether she intends to remain in the United States
or relocate to Ghana or any another country upon denial of the waiver application. The Applicant
must therefore establish that ifhe is denied admission, his spouse and child would experience extreme
hardship both upon separation and relocation.

The Applicant asserts the following in his statement: his presence in the United States is essential to
the financial, medical, and emotional well-being of his spouse, whom he married in 2024, and son,
who was born in 2020; his spouse would experience profound financial instability without him; and
the persistent insecurity would result in a cycle of irreparable hardship for his spouse and son. He
contends that his absence would affect the physical health of his son, who suffers from a chronic ear
condition that required the surgical placement of tubes in both ears, and his spouse, who has
debilitating back problems. He indicates that his daily presence is vital to assist with caregiving and
it would be untenable for his spouse to manage her own medical condition while also shouldering the
financial and medical care responsibilities for their son.

The Applicant's spouse notes the following in her affidavit: she and their son will be subjected to
extreme hardship without the Applicant, and his continued presence is critical to their financial
stability, medical and psychological wellbeing, and family unity. The Applicant owns a tree care and
removal business, his spouse works as a mechanical engineer, and their combined incomes pay their
monthly expenses. His spouse asserts that without the Applicant's monetary support, she would fall
behind on their mortgage and other debt obligations; her damaged credit score would lead to
irreparable instability; and the financial strain would be compounded by her responsibility as a single
parent. She contends that she would be forced to either find a second job, which would require
expensive childcare, or foreclose on their house and default on loans and credit card bills. His spouse
indicates that though they currently depend on her retired parents for school transport and after-school
care, she cannot ask them to do more because they are getting older. His spouse indicates that she and
the Applicant work as a team to support their son and his medical issues, and without the Applicant,
she would shoulder the family's financial obligations while managing unpredictable medical expenses
and caring for their child. His spouse notes that she has chronic medical problems, specifically back
pain and sciatica, which interfere with her mobility and impact her sleep. She receives chiropractic
treatment; frequently relies on the Applicant for caregiving and household tasks; and fears that without
his assistance, she would have no one to help her, her back pain would worsen, and she would be
forced to engage in unsafe physical activity. His spouse contends that the absence of the Applicant's
emotional partnership and parental support, in addition to concern about his wellbeing in Ghana,
would cause her psychological hardship and erode her ability to provide consistent nurturing and care
to their son.

In addition to the statements, the Applicant submitted biographic and civil documents; tax,
employment, education, and financial infmmation; medical records for the Applicant's spouse and
child; support letters; photographs; and articles about Ghana, effects of low credit scores, childcare
availability, details about his spouse's and son's medical conditions, and the impact of parental
absence on children. USCIS denied the waiver application, finding that the evidence was insufficient
to establish the Applicant's spouse and child would experience extreme hardship upon denial of the
waiver application or that a favorable exercise of discretion was warranted. On appeal, the Applicant
asserts USCIS applied an improperly segmented hardship analysis and incorrectly conducted a
discretionary determination without fully considering all positive factors.

Upon review, the Applicant has not overcome USCIS' determination that the evidence is insufficient
to establish extreme hardship. We acknowledge his spouse indicates the Applicant provides her and
their son with emotional, medical, and financial support. However, his spouse also notes that she has
strong ties to her family, community, church, and friends, and is very close with her parents, who live

nearby and provide childcare assistance. Though his spouse indicates her parents are getting older
and she cannot ask them for more help, the record lacks evidence demonstrating her parents are unable
or unwilling to provide additional assistance in the Applicant's absence. Further, the submitted
support letters from his spouse's parents and sisters indicate they all have close relationships with the
Applicant, his spouse, and their son, and frequently spend time together. The record lacks evidence
demonstrating that his spouse's extended family would be unable or unwilling to provide emotional,
financial, or childcare support, or that his spouse would be unable to obtain care from a professional
should she or their son need medical or psychological assistance. Regarding the spouse's claim of
financial hardship, while the evidence documents the Applicant's income contributions to the
household and we acknowledge the spouse may experience some financial difficulty without him, the
record does not demonstrate that she would face a financial strain that would rise to the level of
extreme hardship. For example, the evidence reflects approximate monthly earnings of $3,000 for the
Applicant and $3,655 for his spouse. While his spouse argues that she cannot pay the household
expenses without the Applicant's assistance, the submitted evidence does not establish that the
cumulative financial difficulties resulting from the Applicant's removal would rise to the level of
extreme.

Though the Applicant contends that users failed to consider the aggregate hardship his spouse and
child would suffer, upon review users correctly concluded that the record does not establish that any
hardship his spouse and child would experience, even considered in the aggregate, would rise to the
level of extreme. The Applicant also claims that users failed to adequately analyze the relocation
scenario. However, as previously noted, where there is no statement of intent from the qualifying
relative certifying under penalty of perjury that the qualifying relative would relocate with the
applicant, or would remain in the United States, the applicant must establish that his qualifying relative
would experience extreme hardship both upon separation and relocation. 1 Here, the Applicant's
spouse did not submit a statement indicating whether she would relocate or remain in the United States.
Therefore, as the Applicant did not establish extreme hardship in the event of separation, he has not
met this requirement. In conclusion, while the record demonstrates that the Applicant's spouse and
child may experience emotional, psychological, medical, and financial difficulties due to separation,
the totality of the evidence is insufficient to show the hardship, both individual and cumulative, would
rise beyond the common results of removal or inadmissibility. As this issue is dispositive of the
Applicant's appeal, we do not reach and therefore reserve the issue of whether he would warrant a
favorable exercise of discretion to grant his waiver application. See INS v. Bagamasbad, 429 U.S. 24,
25 (1976) (stating that "courts and agencies are not required to make findings on issues the decision
of which is unnecessary to the results they reach").

ORDER:             The appeal is dismissed.
