Appeal of U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services (USCIS) Decision

Form I-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability)



The Petitioner, a producer and creative director, seeks classification as an alien of extraordinary ability.
See Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S.C. § 1153(b)(l)(A). This first
preference classification makes immigrant visas available to those who can demonstrate their
extraordinary ability through sustained national or international acclaim and whose achievements have
been recognized in their field through extensive documentation.

USCIS denied the petition, concluding the Petitioner did not demonstrate that he satisfied at least three
of the initial evidentiary criteria, as required. Subsequently, USCIS dismissed a combined motion to
reopen and reconsider. The matter is now before us on appeal. 8 C.F.R. § 103.3.

The Petitioner bears the burden of proof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance of the evidence.
Matter afChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter
de novo. Matter a/Christa's, Inc., 26 I&N Dec. 537,537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review,
we will dismiss the appeal.

                                                  I. LAW

Section 203(b )( 1)(A) of the Act makes visas available to immigrants with extraordinary ability if:

        (i)       the alien has extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or
                  athletics which has been demonstrated by sustained national or international
                  acclaim and whose achievements have been recognized in the field through
                  extensive documentation,

        (ii)     the alien seeks to enter the United States to continue work in the area of
                 extraordinary ability, and

        (iii)     the alien's entry into the United States will substantially benefit prospectively
                  the United States.
The term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those individuals in "that small percentage who have
risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). See also Onaghise v. Dep 't of
Homeland Sec., No. 3:20-CV-03033-X, 2022 WL 4073344, at *2 (N.D. Tex. Sept. 2, 2022) (holding
that extraordinary ability visas are known as "Einstein" visas, are extremely "coveted," and are
"reserved for only the most talented" aliens"). The implementing regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)
sets forth a multi-part analysis. First, a petitioner can demonstrate sustained acclaim and the
recognition of achievements in the field through a one-time achievement (that is, a major,
internationally recognized award) or qualifying documentation that meets at least three of the ten
categories listed at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i) - (x) (including items such as awards, published material
in certain media, and scholarly articles).

Where a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, we then consider the totality of the
material provided in a final merits determination and assess whether the record shows sustained
national or international acclaim and demonstrates that the individual is among the small percentage
at the very top of the field of endeavor. See Kazarian v. US. Citizenship & Immigr. Servs., 596 F.3d
1115, 1119-20 (9th Cir. 2010) (discussing a two-part review where the documentation is first counted
and then, if fulfilling the required number of criteria, considered in the context of a final merits
determination); see also Visinscaia v. Beers, 4 F. Supp. 3d 126, 131-32 (D.D.C. 2013); Rijal v. US.
Citizenship & Immigr. Servs., 772 F. Supp. 2d 1339, 1346 (W.D. Wash. 2011), ajf'd, 683 F.3d 1030
(9th Cir. 2012).

                                           II. ANALYSIS

A. Ineffective Assistance of Counsel Claim

The brief asserts:

       To the extent that prior counsel failed to present or explain key evidence and arguments,
       [the Petitioner] respectfully raises ineffective assistance of counsel as an alternative
       ground for relief. Ineffective assistance of counsel is a cognizable claim in immigration
       proceedings, including those seeking discretionary relief Calderon-Rosas v. AG United
       States, 957 F.3d 378 (3d Cir. 2020), Colina v. AG United States, 779 F. App'x 989 (3d
       Cir. 2019). The requirements for raising such a claim are set forth in Matter of Lozada
       and its progeny, including providing an affidavit, notifying former counsel, and
       indicating whether a disciplinary complaint has been filed. If the record demonstrates
       that prior counsel's errors prejudiced the outcome, remand or reopening is warranted.

However, the Petitioner has not complied with the requirements described in Matter of Lozada, 19
I&N Dec. 63 7 (BIA 1988), or established that his former attorney's assistance was so deficient he was
prejudiced by the performance. See Matter ofMelgar, 28 I&N Dec. 169, 171 (BIA 2020). Moreover,
the Petitioner did not explain which "key evidence and arguments" prior counsel purportedly failed to
raise, nor did he offer any documentation to substantiate a Lozada claim.

Because the Petitioner has not claimed or established his receipt of a major, internationally recognized

    1. Published Materials

The Petitioner contests USCIS unfavorable determination for this criterion. Specifically, the brief
asserts:

        The agency's denial hinges on a flawed reading of the second sentence of the criterion.
        The regulation dictates what the petitioner's evidence submission must contain; it does
        not mandate the internal formatting of the published article itself. [The Petitioner's]
        submission clearly identified the title ("For me, the goal that my work serves is primary.
        []"),the date of publication, and the author i.e. the publisher, Bravo.am. The agency's
        demand for an individual journalist's byline within the article is an extra-regulatory
        requirement that the plain language does not support.

The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(iii) requires "[p]]ublished material about the alien in
professional or major trade publications or other major media, relating to the alien's work in the field
for which classification is sought. Such evidence shall include the title, date, and author of the
material, and any necessary translation." 2

We agree with USCIS' determination. The screenshots from bravo.am do not identify the author of
the article. Instead, the introduction simply indicates that the Petitioner "gave an extensive interview
to BRAVO.am." While bravo.am conducted the interview, the evidence does not show who actually
authored the material, as required under 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(iii); see also Nakos v. US. Citizenship
& Immigr. Servs., No. 3:25-CV-0985-D, 2026 WL 2042845, at *5 (N.D. Tex. July 15, 2026) (finding
a distinction between the publisher and author of the material); Javakhadze v. Mayorkas, No. 3:2 l­
CV-00617-E, 2023 WL 8037730, at *6 (N.D. Tex. Nov. 20, 2023) (upholding articles did not contain
the required authors); Limon v. Dep't ofHomeland Sec., No. CV H-25-0063, 2026 WL 2000101, at *4
(S.D. Tex. June 22, 2026) (determining the name of the publication, the name of the author, or the date
of the publication must be included in all articles submitted as they are part of the regulatory
requirements listed at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(iii)); Kumar v. Dir. of US. Citizenship & Immigr. Servs.,
No. CV RDB-23-2251, 2024 WL 1374794, at *5 (D. Md. Apr. 1, 2024) (concluding plaintiff's
evidence did not include the required title, date, or author); Lapteva v. Schuurmans, No. 8:24CV83,
2025 WL 815087, at *4 (D. Neb. Mar. 10, 2025) (indicating petitioner did not provide sufficient
evidence of the required title, date, and author of the material).

We are not persuaded by these assertions. The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(iv) requires
"[e]vidence of the alien's participation, either individually or on a panel, as a judge of the work of
others in the same or an allied field of specification for which classification is sought." The petitioner
must show that the person has not only been invited to judge the work of others, but also that the
person actually participated in the judging of the work of others in the same or an allied field of
specialization. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, at F.2(B)(l ).

Here, the Petitioner makes speculations and assumptions without supporting the record. The Petitioner
did not demonstrate that his field of producing and creative directing allies with singing. The
Petitioner does not point to submitted evidence, nor does the record contain documentation
corroborating any of his assertions. See S-M-, 22 I&N Dec. at 51; MRC Energy Co. v. US. Citizenship
& Immigr. Servs., No. 3:19-CV-2003-K, 2021 WL 1209188, at *6 (N.D. Tex. Mar. 31, 2021) (finding
the plaintiff's burden to identify specific items in the record to support its position).

The Petitioner bears the burden of proof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance of the evidence.
Chawathe, 25 I&N Dec. at 375-76. In this case, the Petitioner must show that he participated "as a
judge of the work of others in the same or an allied field." Thus, in order to demonstrate eligibility
for this criterion, the Petitioner must establish that his participation on the selection committee for the
  ________                   was either in the same or an allied field of his specialization, producing and
creative directing. However, he did not do so. The Petitioner did not show that the singing contest
involved producing or creative directing or is an allied field to them. See, e.g., Sarin v. United States,
No. 2:22-CV-07498-SVW-JS, 2023 WL 5667531, at *11 (C.D. Cal. July 27, 2023) (upholding
plaintiff's judging of an intra-school personality contest was not in the field of acting or in an allied

    4. Summary

The Petitioner did not establish eligibility for any of the categories of evidence discussed above.
Because the Petitioner cannot fulfill the initial evidentiary requirement of at least three under 8 C.F.R.
§ 204.5(h)(3), we need not review ofUSCIS' favorable determination for the roles and salary criteria.
See Kinuthia v. Eiden, 702 F. Supp. 3d 5, 11 (D. Mass. 2023), ajf'd sub nom. Kinuthia v. Trump, No.
23-2029, 2025 WL 3491324 (1st Cir. Aug. 12, 2025) (declining to consider a criterion when the
plaintiff cannot satisfy at least three); see also INS v. Bagamasbad, 429 U.S. 24, 25 (1976) (per curiam)
(holding that agencies are not required to make "purely advisory findings" on issues that are
unnecessary to the ultimate decision).

                                           III. CONCLUSION

Congress set a very high benchmark for aliens of extraordinary ability by requiring the petitioner
demonstrate "sustained national or international acclaim" and present "extensive documentation" to
show the beneficiary's achievements have been recognized in the field. Section 203(b )( 1)(A)(i) of the
Act. Because the Petitioner has not satisfied the threshold "initial evidence" requirements at 8 C.F.R.
§ 204.5(h)(3), we cannot conclude that he is an alien of extraordinary ability and need not further
analyze the merits under the statute. See Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 1119-20; see also Nagaiah v. Allen,
784 F. Supp. 3d 798, 814 (E.D. Pa. 2025) (finding the regulation "establishes a necessary, not
