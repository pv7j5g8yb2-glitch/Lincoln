### 2. Requirement: qualifying managerial capacity in the United States (INA 101(a)(44)(A); 8 CFR 204.5(j))
Assessment: not met on the present record. The petition is framed as managerial only (p. 1). Managerial capacity requires, in substance, that the beneficiary manage the organization or a department, subdivision or function, or supervise and control the work of other supervisory, professional or managerial employees. It also requires authority over personnel actions or equivalent discretion, and primary assignment to managerial rather than operational tasks.

Staffing. Five employees are reported at filing: a sales manager, two sales associates, a warehouse operator and a showroom operator, all said to report directly or indirectly to the beneficiary (p. 1). None of the subordinate positions requires a bachelor's degree (p. 1). The record therefore does not show that he supervises professionals. Any managerial claim must rest on his supervising other supervisory or managerial employees, or on managing the organization itself. The only candidate is the sales manager, whose job description is reported to overlap with the duties of the sales associates (p. 1). [Attorney: not shown on p. 1.] That overlap undercuts treating the position as supervisory. [Attorney: decide whether to argue that the sales manager is a genuine supervisor, and obtain evidence of his actual authority.]

Outside contractors. An accounting firm is paid $350 to $400 a month for bookkeeping, payroll and tax returns (p. 1). Installation contractors are also used, and marketing consultants were used in 2019 and 2020 but none at filing (p. 1). The reliance on contractors for core financial functions leaves the beneficiary's own role unclear. The duties letter says he manages payroll and coordinates with the company's accountant (p. 1). It does not show whether he performs those tasks himself or oversees others who do.

Duties letter. The letter lists establishing and developing operational and financial systems and policies, controlling the budget, managing payroll, salaries and expenses, supervising and coaching the administrative staff, planning and monitoring receipt, assembly and dispatch of goods, recruiting, training and monitoring staff with full discretion to fire, and reviewing reports prepared by the Financial Manager (p. 1). Several items read as operational: organizing financial documents, reconciling monthly activity, using warehouse space efficiently, and ensuring staff update stock control systems (p. 1). The letter gives no percentages of time (p. 1). It is generic and does not tie duties to named subordinates. The petition cannot show that the primary assignment is managerial without time allocation and staff linkage.

Essential function. The petitioner claims no essential function (p. 1). The function-manager alternative is therefore not available unless the theory is revised. [Attorney: decide whether to add a function-manager theory for finance and operations. The size of the staff and the use of an outside accountant make that theory difficult.]

No such position appears among the five employees, and bookkeeping, payroll and tax returns are done by an outside accounting firm (p. 1). Administrative staff. The letter claims he supervises and coaches the administrative staff (p. 1). No administrative employee appears among the five employees listed (p. 1). The claim is unsupported as written.

Organizational chart. The summary states that there is no organizational chart yet (p. 1). It also states that the chart shows the warehouse operator supervising the installation contractors (p. 1). These statements conflict. [Attorney: confirm whether a chart exists, who prepared it and when.]

Warehouse operator. The warehouse operator's job description lists no project or supervisory tasks, although the chart shows him supervising the installation contractors (p. 1). Contractors are not employees, and the supervision claim lacks a documented source.

Discretion to fire. The letter claims full discretion to fire (p. 1). The reporting lines are inconsistent, as shown above, and the claim must be reconciled with them before it is relied on.

Florida showroom. All work is at the main location and no one is listed at the Florida showroom (p. 1). The record does not show who operates that showroom or under whose supervision. This bears on the claimed structure of the business.
