### 2. Requirement: Qualifying Criminal Activity
Governing rule: INA 101(a)(15)(U)(iii) lists qualifying criminal activity, including felonious assault; the implementing regulation is 8 CFR 214.14. The statute does not list robbery or attempted robbery as qualifying.

Evidence: One Supplement B describes the petitioner as a victim of felonious assault and attempted robbery (p. 1). A second Supplement B cites California Penal Code 211 (robbery) and 245(a) (assault with a deadly weapon) and states that two masked men entered the restaurant where he worked, assaulted him and other employees with a gun and demanded money (p. 1). The police report describes an attempted robbery in which two masked men pointed a gun at his son and demanded the safe be opened (p. 1).

Assessment: Partly met, with weakness. The certification relies on assault. The police report describes a gun pointed and the men pushing the victims, and records that they did not strike them (p. 1). Whether this supports felonious assault depends on how the accounts are reconciled. [Attorney: decide whether the facts as documented support felonious assault, or whether another listed offense or similar activity can be argued.]

### 3. Requirement: Law Enforcement Certification (Supplement B)

### 4. Requirement: Victim of Qualifying Activity Who Suffered Substantial Physical or Mental Abuse
Governing rule: INA 101(a)(15)(U)(i)(I); 8 CFR 214.14(b)(1). The factors are the nature of the injury, the severity of the perpetrator's conduct, the severity of the harm, the duration of the harm, and the permanent or serious harm to appearance, health or physical or mental soundness. No single factor is required.

Evidence supporting the requirement: The Supplement B injury section states that he was extremely frightened throughout the assault (p. 1). Urgent care, 12 days after the incident, recorded anxiety, insomnia and palpitations several times a day, diagnosed PTSD, prescribed anti-anxiety medication and referred him to a psychologist (p. 1). His 2017 statement describes emotional trauma, a doctor and a psychologist, and medication for a few months; a later statement says a gun was pointed at his head, he thought they would all die, he became afraid to go out and feared strangers, and still feels the effects (p. 1). His son states that he became solitary, stopped taking his grandchildren to the park and attended therapy until workers' compensation stopped paying (p. 1). The April 2025 psychologist diagnoses moderate, chronic PTSD with flashbacks, night terrors and avoidance (p. 1).

Evidence undercutting the requirement: The police report records that the men left about a minute and a half after entering and that no injuries were recorded (p. 1). Nine days after the urgent care visit, symptoms were several times a week, and the workers' compensation form states that he was able to do his usual work without restrictions (p. 1). The 2012 psychologist, after four sessions, found adjustment disorder with anxiety, stated that he calmed down and continued the scheduled shift after the robbers left, and found that he copes relatively well, is anxious in an expected fashion, keeps up enjoyable activities and is somewhat worried for his family (p. 1). The 2016 letter is summarized with the 2012 report (p. 1). The 2025 report states that he and his wife now run a successful restaurant, that his biggest problems are his wife's health and his immigration status, and that he copes with walks and talking; the psychologist also notes that his checklist score is higher than his own account suggests (p. 1).

Inconsistencies bearing on credibility: (a) The police report states the men did not strike them but pushed them (p. 1); the 2025 psychologist states that he reports the men hit him, his wife and son with the pistol and threatened to kill them (p. 1). The striking account appears in no earlier document summarized in the file. (b) The 2017 statement says both men had guns, one pointed at his son and one at him (p. 1); the police report says a gun was pointed at his son and that video shows one man with a bag and one with a gun (p. 1). (c) The 2017 statement and urgent care record show prescription anti-anxiety medication for a few months (p. 1) [Attorney: not shown on p. 1.]; the 2025 report states he has never taken psychiatric medication (p. 1). (d) Urgent care diagnosed PTSD at 12 days (p. 1); the 2012 and 2016 psychologist found adjustment disorder (p. 1).

Assessment: Not presently met on this record, and the weakest requirement. Physical abuse is not shown: no injuries were recorded and the only contact documented is pushing (p. 1). Mental abuse has some support from the urgent care diagnosis, the son's account of his withdrawal and the 2025 report, but the contemporaneous psychologist findings, the absence of work restrictions, the brief duration, the short treatment, the late and inconsistent 2025 account and the successful business all weigh against substantial harm. Note that the severity of the threat (a gun pointed at his head, if credited) is a separate point in his favor. [Attorney: decide whether to pursue a detailed client declaration reconciling the accounts, and whether to commission a new expert evaluation; the 2025 report as it stands may harm the case because it conflicts with earlier records.]
