### U-1 Eligibility Assessment: Substantial Physical or Mental Abuse and Related Issues (Prospective Client, Mexican National) (for_review)
### Summary of the File and Scope
This assessment is prepared for the attorney's review. The file consists of a firm summary of the evidence, not the underlying documents (p. 1). The summary states that a Mexican man (the Applicant) filed Form I-918, Petition for U Nonimmigrant Status, in 2017 and that USCIS asked for more evidence (p. 1). The task is to assess whether the Applicant can show substantial physical or mental abuse and to identify other problems (p. 2).

The Applicant's name, A-Number, entry history, arrests and the current status of the I-918 are not in the file. The statements below rest on the evidence as the summary describes it. The burden is on the Applicant, by a preponderance of the evidence. Matter of Chawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369 (AAO 2010).

The governing statute for the requirements below is INA § 101(a)(15)(U)(i) and, for the regulatory definition of substantial abuse, 8 C.F.R. § 214.14(b)(1). The regulation lists factors including the nature of the injury, the severity of the perpetrator's conduct, the severity of the harm, the duration of the harm, and the extent of permanent or serious harm to appearance, health, or physical or mental soundness. No single factor is required. The citations are given from Lincoln's general knowledge of the law and the attorney should verify them before use.

### Requirement 1: Qualifying Criminal Activity
Finding: uncertain, and not established on the present record.

One Supplement B (Form I-918, Supplement B, U Nonimmigrant Status Certification) describes the Applicant as a victim of felonious assault and attempted robbery (p. 1). A second Supplement B cites California Penal Code § 211 (robbery) and § 245(a) (assault with a deadly weapon) and describes two masked men who entered the restaurant where he worked, assaulted him and other employees with a gun, and demanded money (p. 1). Robbery is not itself an enumerated qualifying crime, so eligibility would rest on felonious assault or a similar enumerated offense. Attempted robbery alone would not suffice.

The 2012 police report records an attempted robbery in which two masked men pointed a gun at the Applicant's son and demanded that the safe be opened (p. 1). The Applicant told police that the men 'did not strike them but did push them to move them around', and no injuries were recorded (p. 1). The report as summarized does not show a pointed gun directed at the Applicant or an assault on him. The certifier's description of the offense does not bind USCIS and must be tested against the police report.

### Requirement 2: Direct Victim Status
Finding: weak, and in conflict across the file.

The accounts of the incident differ. The 2012 police report records one gun pointed at the son and the Applicant and son on the floor with hands up (p. 1). The Applicant's 2017 statement says both men had guns, one pointed at his son and one at him (p. 1). His later statement says a gun was pointed at his head (p. 1). The April 2025 psychologist records that the Applicant says the men hit him, his wife and his son with the pistol (p. 1). The report records no striking (p. 1).

The Applicant would be a direct victim if he was the target of an assault with a gun. If the contemporaneous report is credited, the gun was directed at the son and the Applicant was pushed. The inconsistency is material and unexplained in the file, and it damages credibility. ### Requirement 3: Substantial Physical or Mental Abuse
Finding: not met on the present record; the prospects on this element are poor.

Physical injury. No injuries were recorded in the 2012 police report (p. 1). The men left about a minute and a half after entering (p. 1). The urgent care visit twelve days after the incident found no work restrictions, and the workers' compensation form states that he was able to do his usual work without restrictions (p. 1).

Contemporaneous mental harm. Urgent care, twelve days after the incident, recorded anxiety, insomnia and palpitations several times a day, a PTSD diagnosis, anti-anxiety medication and a referral to a psychologist (p. 1). Nine days later symptoms were several times a week (p. 1). The psychologist's November 2012 report and 2016 letter, after four sessions, record that after the robbers left the family calmed down and continued their scheduled shift, and diagnose an adjustment disorder with anxiety (p. 1). The psychologist states that he copes 'relatively well', is 'anxious in an expected fashion', keeps up enjoyable activities and is 'somewhat worried' for his family (p. 1). These records indicate mild and diminishing symptoms and a diagnosis short of PTSD.

Supporting evidence. The first Supplement B injury section states that he 'was extremely frightened throughout the assault' (p. 1). The Applicant's 2017 statement describes emotional trauma, a doctor, a psychologist and prescription medication for a few months (p. 1). His later statement says that he thought they would all die, became afraid to go out, feared strangers and still feels the effects (p. 1). The son's statement says that the Applicant became solitary, stopped taking his grandchildren to the park and attended therapy until workers' compensation stopped paying (p. 1). The certifier's wording on injury is not a finding binding on USCIS. These statements are largely the Applicant's own or from family, and the psychologist's contemporaneous records do not corroborate lasting impairment.

The 2025 evaluation. The April 2025 psychologist diagnoses moderate, chronic PTSD with flashbacks, night terrors and avoidance, and notes that his checklist score is higher than his own account suggests (p. 1). The evaluation conflicts with the 2012 and 2016 findings and describes the men as hitting the family, which the police report does not record (p. 1). It post-dates the 2017 filing, and the file does not show whether it was submitted to USCIS. Evidence of later events cannot cure a petition that was not eligible at filing. 8 C.F.R. § 103.2(b)(1). It also records that he has never taken psychiatric medication, which contradicts the 2017 statement and the urgent care records (p. 1). It further records that he and his wife now run a successful restaurant, that his biggest problems are his wife's health and his immigration status, and that he copes with walks and talking (p. 1). On this description, the current stressors are not tied to the crime.

Analysis. Applying the factors of 8 C.F.R. § 214.14(b)(1), the nature of the harm was threatened violence with a firearm, which is serious in kind. The duration was very short, no physical injury is recorded, the symptoms in the contemporaneous records were transient or moderate, and the permanent or serious harm factor is supported only by the 2025 evaluation, which is internally and externally inconsistent. On a preponderance standard, the record does not currently show substantial mental abuse. If the 2025 evaluation is credited and the contemporaneous PTSD diagnosis at urgent care is weighed with it, a showing is conceivable, but the contradictions on medication, the manner of the assault and the course of symptoms would be central to any credibility finding. If this element fails, the case cannot succeed, and the remaining elements would not be reached in any event.
