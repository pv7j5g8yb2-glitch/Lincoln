### 3. Requirement Two: Extreme Hardship to the Qualifying Relative
Governing rule: extreme hardship must go beyond the usual consequences of removal or separation, Matter of Cervantes-Gonzalez, 22 I&N Dec. 560 (BIA 1999), and Matter of Pilch, 21 I&N Dec. 627 (BIA 1996). Factors are considered individually and in the aggregate, Matter of Ige, 20 I&N Dec. 880 (BIA 1994). The wife has not stated whether she would remain in the US or move to the Bahamas if the waiver is denied (p. 1). Without a sworn statement of her intent, hardship must be shown in both scenarios, and failure as to either fails the requirement (USCIS Policy Manual, Volume 9, Part B).

Separation scenario. The wife's affidavit states that she has high blood pressure and anxiety, is on medication, and relies on the Applicant to manage her stress, watch her blood pressure and care for her when she is unwell (p. 1). Their son, born in 2017, has autism and ADHD and needs constant supervision, speech therapy three times a week, occupational therapy twice a week and daily home schooling (p. 1). The wife is a full time high school teacher. The Applicant home schools the boy Monday to Friday, takes him to therapy and works full time evenings and overnight (p. 1). The wife states that without him she could not afford the home, the therapies or living costs and would have to cut hours or resign (p. 1). A February 2026 psychologist's evaluation diagnoses hypertension and generalized anxiety disorder, medicated, caused by the Applicant's immigration situation, and concludes that single parenting a special needs child while working full time would likely exceed her emotional capacity (p. 1). The treating physician's letter states that both conditions are chronic and need ongoing management (p. 1). The Applicant's affidavit states that he is the boy's primary caregiver and contributes his full time income (p. 1). The file also lists extensive medical and school records for the son naming the Applicant as instructor, and proof of his employment and income (p. 1).

Assessment of separation. The record points one way and nothing in it points the other. Medical, psychological, financial and caregiving factors, taken in the aggregate, are likely to go beyond the usual consequences of separation. Finding: likely met, with the following weaknesses. First, the son is not a qualifying relative, and his hardship counts only as it affects the wife. Second, the summary does not state whether the son is the wife's child, her biological child or the Applicant's, and the relationship is not documented. Third, the summary describes the Applicant as home schooling Monday to Friday while working full time evenings and overnight, and the wife as working full time as a teacher. The caregiving schedule needs to be confirmed and made consistent. Fourth, the psychologist attributes the wife's conditions to the immigration situation, while the summary also describes them as chronic. A reviewer may read this as inconsistent or as a cause that predates the case. The treating physician's letter and the psychologist's evaluation should be reconciled on onset and cause. Fifth, the summary does not address whether other family members or paid help could provide support, a factor that adjudicators consider. Sixth, the affidavits come from the spouses, who are interested parties. Corroboration from therapists, teachers and the physician carries more weight, and the financial claim needs household budget and therapy cost documents.

Relocation scenario. The wife states that she was born and raised in the US, owns a home, has no ties to the Bahamas, would lose her career, and that the Bahamas lacks the specialized therapy and education the boy needs, with poor healthcare access, few specialists, medication shortages and high costs (p. 1). These claims are asserted without any cited source in the summary. No country conditions reports are listed, and no deed or mortgage statement supports the home ownership. Finding: likely met on the facts as described. The claims about conditions in the Bahamas are the wife's statements and are unsupported until objective reports are obtained. [Attorney: obtain country conditions evidence on specialist therapy, autism services, education and medication supply in the Bahamas, and evidence on the availability of the wife's medication there.]

Overall on hardship. Because both scenarios are addressed, the absence of the wife's statement of intent is not fatal as the file stands. The separation case is thinner than the relocation case as to documents and carries the inconsistencies noted above. [Attorney: decide whether to obtain a sworn statement of intent from the wife. A statement that she would remain in the US narrows the case to separation, and a statement that she would relocate narrows it to relocation. Presenting both is the safer course unless the facts clearly favor one.] Hardship to the Applicant himself is not counted and must not be argued as a factor.
