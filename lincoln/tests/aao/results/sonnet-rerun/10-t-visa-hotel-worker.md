### Requirement 1: Victim of a Severe Form of Trafficking in Persons
Governing rule: INA § 101(a)(15)(T)(i)(I); the definition of a severe form of trafficking in 22 U.S.C. § 7102. For labor trafficking, the Applicant must show recruitment, harboring, transportation, provision or obtaining of a person for labor or services through force, fraud or coercion for the purpose of subjection to involuntary servitude, peonage, debt bondage or slavery.

Act and purpose. The Applicant was recruited by D-H- and the N-P- Agency and entered the United States as an H-2B hotel housekeeper in December 2005, November 2007 and October 2009 (p. 1). The labor was performed at a Florida hotel (p. 1).

Fraud. The Applicant states that recruiters used false promises, and that she did not receive the promised wages, benefits, hours or room and board (p. 1). She states that her family took large loans against the family farm and land to pay recruitment fees (p. 1).

Coercion and debt. She states that she was told at orientation that she could be deported for breaking rules and was asked for her passport, and that she gave a copy (p. 1). She states that she was pushed to work more in less time and was repeatedly threatened with arrest and deportation by the hotels, D-H- and the agency if she stopped working (p. 1). She states that in fall 2007 she was threatened with deportation or jail unless she went home, paid a $3,000 renewal fee and a $2,000 return fee, and borrowed $3,000 from an aunt against the family farm, home and land (p. 1). She states that she wanted to stay but was told she had no choice, and that the agency contacted her at least five times, warning at least three times that she would never work in the United States again (p. 1). She states that in 2009 she was threatened with being 'blacklisted' and that voicemails said she would be held at the airport if she did not pay (p. 1). She states that she escaped in April 2010 because she could no longer pay placement fees and flights (p. 1).

Finding: likely met, as described. The account describes recruitment through false promises, debt secured against family property, repeated threats of legal harm, and a continuing demand for payment tied to continued work, which together fit a debt bondage and coercion theory. The theory rests largely on the Applicant's own account. Corroboration is listed only in general terms: employment contracts, temporary job offer letters and documents from the recruiters (p. 1). The summary does not show that these documents are in the file. Passport retention is weak, because she states that only a copy was given (p. 1). The strength of the coercion element will turn on the voicemails, payment receipts, loan and land records and the declaration, none of which is in the file.

[Attorney: Decide whether the facts support involuntary servitude or debt bondage as the labor trafficking theory, and whether the passport copy and threats of deportation are sufficient coercion without physical confinement.]

### Requirement 2: Physical Presence in the United States on Account of Trafficking
Governing rule: INA § 101(a)(15)(T)(i)(II). The Applicant must be physically present in the United States on account of the trafficking, which includes presence because of an initial entry or because she was subjected to trafficking while present. The implementing regulation addresses presence after an opportunity to leave, and it must be confirmed and applied by the attorney; no specific paragraph is cited here.

The file is deficient on this requirement. The Applicant entered three times, departed and returned in 2007 and 2009, and each return was to the same Florida hotel after fees were paid (p. 1). She escaped in April 2010 (p. 1). The next documented event is the December 2020 evaluation and program enrollment (p. 1). The file contains no statement of where she lived, her status or her whereabouts after April 2010, and no statement that she remains in the United States today.

The 2007 and 2009 departures cut against continuous presence, but they were arguably coerced by the same scheme: she states that she was threatened with deportation or jail unless she went home and that she wanted to stay (p. 1). The returns were made under continuing debt and threats (p. 1). Her trafficking is therefore described as continuing across the entries. The more serious difficulty is the period from April 2010 onward, over sixteen years before today, with no explanation in the file for why she remained and no evidence of ties to the trafficking in that period.

Finding: not established on the current file. Present physical presence cannot be assessed, and the link between the 2010 escape and her present presence is unexplained. The requirement may be met if the Applicant can show, by declaration and records, that she is in the United States and that her presence relates to the trafficking, for example because of the debts, fear of the traffickers, trauma or her cooperation. Attorney judgment is needed on whether the long gap defeats the claim.

[Attorney: Obtain the Applicant's residence, status and employment history since April 2010, and her explanation for remaining in the United States. Decide whether the 2007 and 2009 departures and returns affect the analysis, and whether the gap can be explained by trauma, debt or continuing fear.]

### Requirement 3: Compliance with Reasonable Requests from Law Enforcement

### Admissibility, Bars and Prior History
Governing rule: INA § 212(a)(9)(B) and (C). Each entry and departure must be confirmed. The file gives no I-94 records, no admission end dates and no status after April 2010. If she remained after her H-2B authorization ended, she may have accrued unlawful presence, and if it exceeded a year followed by a departure and an entry without inspection, the permanent bar could be implicated. The file does not show any such facts. Unlawful presence provisions contain an exception for trafficking victims where the trafficking is a central reason for the presence, and a T application also allows a waiver of inadmissibility under INA § 212(d)(3) and (d)(13), which the attorney must confirm. Whether this exception applies depends on the same connection that is unresolved under Requirement 2.

The file is silent on arrests, court cases, prior applications, removal orders and family members. Criminal grounds cannot be assessed. The consultation rulebook requires that these be confirmed, including through FOIA requests to USCIS, CBP and EOIR, before advice is given or anything is filed.
