### Requirement 1: Qualifying Relationship Between the Foreign and US Entities
Governing rule: INA § 203(b)(1)(C); 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(j)(3)(i)(C), which requires that the US employer be the same employer, or a parent, branch, subsidiary or affiliate, of the foreign employer.

Finding: not determinable on the file. The case note identifies no foreign entity, no ownership or control, and no evidence of the relationship (p. 1). The prior L-1A approvals are history in another visa class and do not bind USCIS; they cannot be counted as support. This requirement is not shown, and the case cannot succeed without it. [Attorney: Obtain the foreign entity's organizational documents, stock records and ownership evidence for both entities.]

### Requirement 2: One Year of Qualifying Employment Abroad
Governing rule: INA § 203(b)(1)(C); 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(j)(3)(i)(A) and (B), which requires at least one year of employment abroad in a managerial or executive capacity within the three years before entry as a nonimmigrant, and a US position that is also managerial or executive.

Finding: not shown. The case note says nothing of the Beneficiary's employment abroad, its dates or its capacity (p. 1). The foreign employer's continued doing business is also undocumented. ### Requirement 3: Employment in a Managerial Capacity in the United States
Governing rule: INA § 101(a)(44)(A) defines managerial capacity as managing the organization, or a department, subdivision, function or component of it; supervising and controlling the work of other supervisory, professional or managerial employees, or managing an essential function; having authority to hire and fire or recommend those actions, or functioning at a senior level within the organizational hierarchy or with respect to the function managed; and exercising discretion over day-to-day operations. A first-line supervisor is not a manager unless the supervised employees are professionals. The Petitioner claims employment as a manager of personnel only, and claims no essential function (p. 1). [Attorney: not shown on p. 1.]

Finding: not met on the described record.

Staffing. At filing the Petitioner had five employees, all reporting directly or indirectly to the Beneficiary: a sales manager, two sales associates, a warehouse operator and a showroom operator (p. 1). None of the subordinate positions requires a bachelor's degree (p. 1). The record therefore shows no professional subordinates. Whether any subordinate is a true supervisor or manager is doubtful. The sales manager's job description shows sales duties overlapping with the sales associates (p. 1), which weakens the claim that this position supervises and relieves the Beneficiary of non-managerial work. The warehouse operator is shown on the chart as supervising the installation contractors, but the job description lists no project or supervisory tasks (p. 1). The installation contractors are contractors and not employees (p. 1). The organizational chart does not yet exist (p. 1). On this record, the Beneficiary is likely to be read as a first-line supervisor of non-professional staff.

Duties. The duties letter describes establishing and developing financial and operational systems and policies, controlling the budget, managing payroll, salaries and expenses, organizing financial documents, supervising and coaching administrative staff, coordinating with the company's accountant, reconciling monthly activity, year end reports and tax requirements, planning and monitoring receipt, assembly and dispatch of goods, using warehouse space efficiently, ensuring staff update stock control systems, planning capacity, and recruiting, training and monitoring staff with full discretion to fire (p. 1). Several of these duties read as tasks performed rather than managed, including payroll, organizing financial documents, reconciling activity and stock control. The letter gives no percentages of time (p. 1). Without a breakdown, USCIS cannot determine how much time is spent on managerial duties. The letter itself is not in the file, and the description of the hire and fire authority is unverified. No personnel records show any hiring or firing decision by the Beneficiary.

Outsourcing conflict. An accounting firm is paid $350 to $400 a month for bookkeeping, payroll and tax returns (p. 1). This conflicts with the duties letter's claim that the Beneficiary manages payroll and reconciles financial activity and tax requirements (p. 1). Either the Beneficiary performs these tasks, which is operational, or the firm does, which undercuts the claim. The conflict must be reconciled.

Unidentified Financial Manager. The duties letter states that the Beneficiary reviews reports prepared by the 'Financial Manager' (p. 1). No employee with that title appears among the five subordinates (p. 1), and bookkeeping is outsourced (p. 1). The identity of this person is unexplained. [Attorney: Determine whether the title refers to the accountant, the sales manager or an unlisted person, and correct the letter.]

Florida showroom. No one is listed at the Florida showroom, and all work is at the main location (p. 1). Who operates that showroom, and the Beneficiary's role there, is unexplained.

Adequacy of staffing. Two showrooms and a warehouse are run by five employees, with no marketing consultants at filing (p. 1). Consultants were used in 2019 and 2020 (p. 1). USCIS may question whether the Beneficiary is relieved from operational duties. Reasonable need must be considered in light of the Petitioner's size and stage, but the record offers no corroboration.
