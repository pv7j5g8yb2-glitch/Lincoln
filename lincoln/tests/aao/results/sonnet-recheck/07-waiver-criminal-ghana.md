### Eligibility Assessment: Form I-601 Waivers under INA 212(h) and 212(i), Ghanaian Husband (Prospective Client) (for_review)
### 1. Posture and scope
This assessment is prepared for Attorney Alex Rivera at an initial consultation. The file is a one-page summary, not the underlying documents. Findings below weigh the evidence as described. The standard of proof is a preponderance of the evidence (Matter of Chawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369 (AAO 2010)).

The case concerns a Ghanaian man who is inadmissible under INA 212(a)(2)(A) for two convictions for crimes involving moral turpitude and under 212(a)(6)(C)(i) for misrepresenting his criminal history, and who does not contest either finding (p. 1). He seeks waivers under 212(h) and 212(i) (p. 1). The matter is at consultation. No I-601, filing or approval is in the file. The case is waiting on the attorney's review and on the client for documents.

[Attorney: The file does not state the client's full name, the underlying application the I-601 would support, whether he is in the US or abroad, or how and when the findings were made. Confirm the pathway (for example I-485 or consular immigrant visa) and the correct form (I-601, or I-601A if he is abroad).]

### 2. Inadmissibility grounds and need for both waivers
The client does not contest the 212(a)(2)(A) finding or the 212(a)(6)(C)(i) finding (p. 1). Finding: both grounds are conceded for purposes of this assessment.

A 212(h) waiver does not cover the misrepresentation ground, and a 212(i) waiver does not cover the criminal ground. Both waivers are therefore required, and denial of either ends the case. The client cannot be admitted or adjusted if either ground remains unwaived.

### 3. Qualifying relative
Under 212(i), the only qualifying relatives are a US citizen or lawful permanent resident spouse or parent. The file states that the client's wife is a US citizen and that they married in 2024 (p. 1). The file also states that the couple has a US citizen son born in 2020 (p. 1). The son is not a qualifying relative under 212(i), so hardship to him counts for 212(i) only as it affects the wife.

Under 212(h)(1)(B), a qualifying relative is a US citizen or lawful permanent resident spouse, parent, son or daughter. The wife and the son both qualify on the facts stated (p. 1).

Finding: likely met, based on the summary. No marriage certificate, birth certificate or proof of the wife's or son's citizenship is in the file. [Attorney: Obtain these documents. Confirm the marriage is bona fide and that the client's relationship to the son is documented.]

### 4. Section 212(h) waiver for the criminal ground
Section 212(h)(1)(A) requires, among other things, that the activity occurred more than 15 years before the application. The summary states that the convictions are not more than 15 years old (p. 1). On the stated facts, this route is unavailable. Finding: not met. [Attorney: Confirm once offense dates are known.]

Section 212(h)(1)(B) requires that refusal of admission would cause extreme hardship to a qualifying relative, and the waiver is discretionary. The file does not state the offenses, dates, sentences or rehabilitation. These facts are needed for the discretionary balance and for any assessment of the nature of the crimes. Finding on this route: the hardship requirement is addressed in Section 6; the discretionary step cannot be assessed on this file.

### 5. Section 212(i) waiver for misrepresentation
Section 212(i) requires extreme hardship to the US citizen or permanent resident spouse or parent, and is also discretionary. Hardship to the son does not count independently. The file does not state what was misrepresented, to whom, when, or whether it was material. These facts are missing. [Attorney: Obtain the misrepresentation facts and any prior applications, refusals or removal history.]

### 6. Extreme hardship to the wife
Extreme hardship must go beyond the usual consequences of removal or separation, considered individually and together. The wife has not stated whether she would remain in the US or relocate to Ghana (p. 1). Hardship must therefore be shown in both scenarios, and failure in either fails the requirement.

Facts in the wife's affidavit, as her own statements: she states that the client owns a tree care and removal business earning about $3,000 a month, that she is a mechanical engineer earning about $3,655 a month, and that together they cover the mortgage and debts (p. 1). She states that without him she would fall behind, damage her credit, need a second job and costly childcare, or face foreclosure (p. 1). She states that her retired parents live nearby and do school runs and after school care but are getting older (p. 1). She states that she has chronic back pain and sciatica, receives chiropractic care, and relies on him for caregiving and housework (p. 1). She states that she has strong ties to family, community, church and friends (p. 1). The client states that the son has a chronic ear condition that needed tubes in both ears and that the wife has debilitating back problems (p. 1). Support letters from her parents and sisters describe close relationships and frequent time together (p. 1).

Scenario one, separation (wife stays in the US): the evidence describes financial strain, loss of childcare and caregiving help, and medical conditions. Her income is stated to be higher than his, and her parents live nearby and provide childcare, which weakens the claim of severe financial and childcare hardship. Financial loss, credit damage and single parenting are common consequences of separation. Her back condition is stated only in affidavits, with no medical evidence described as to severity or need for his help. Finding: not shown to exceed the usual consequences of separation on the file as described, so likely not met on its own. The records would be needed to change this view.

Scenario two, relocation (wife moves to Ghana): the file lists articles on Ghana and the medical conditions only as evidence (p. 1). Nothing describes her ties to Ghana, language, employment prospects, availability of treatment for her back condition or the son's ear condition, or schooling for the son. Finding: not met on this file, because no relocation hardship is described. Failing either scenario fails the requirement, so the extreme hardship finding is currently not met.

Weight: the hardship claims rest largely on statements by the client, the wife and relatives, who are interested parties. The medical records, tax, financial and credit records are listed (p. 1) but are not in the file. The wife's income figures need pay and tax records.

### 7. Discretion
Hardship is only the threshold. Discretion is a separate step that weighs the grounds of inadmissibility and other adverse factors against the equities. Adverse factors are two moral turpitude convictions and a misrepresentation about the criminal history. Equities described are the US citizen wife and son, a business, family ties and, as stated by the wife, community and church ties (p. 1). No evidence of rehabilitation, such as completion of sentence, probation compliance, character references or the client's own statement of remorse, is described beyond the client's statement on family presence (p. 1). Finding: cannot be assessed; the misrepresentation concerning the criminal history is a significant adverse factor.

### 8. Overall view and prospects
On the file as described, the case is weak. The qualifying relationships are likely met. The 212(h)(1)(A) route is not available. The case rests on extreme hardship to the wife, which is currently not met because relocation hardship is not addressed and separation hardship appears to be within the usual consequences. Because both waivers require this hardship, the case cannot succeed without it, and the discretionary step is not reached unless it is established. The prospects improve only if the wife commits to a stated scenario and documented evidence shows hardship beyond the usual, in particular the son's and her own medical needs and conditions in Ghana.

Other grounds have not been screened. The misrepresentation about criminal history suggests a prior application, and the file does not rule out further inadmissibility grounds, unlawful presence or bars to adjustment.
